consentvid

Open-source software and a record format for video datasets in which every clip has an explicit consent record. The record lives in the contributor’s own Bluesky (ATproto) account, states what the video may be used for, by whom and until when, and is deleted to withdraw consent.

Version 0.1.0. No dataset has been released yet.

Give consent for a video

Consent records are public. Withdrawing stops new use of a video, not models already trained on it.

Four ways to contribute

Each way ends with the same two records in your account: a video record and a consent record. The ways differ in what you need and in where the video file stays.

01

Consent page: Bluesky posts

For videos you already posted on Bluesky.

  1. Open consentvid.org/app and enter your handle. You approve on your own server. The page asks for exactly this permission:

    atproto repo:org.consentvid.videoV2?action=create&action=delete repo:org.consentvid.consentV2?action=create&action=delete blob:video/*

    If your server does not support these permissions, the page asks you before requesting a broader one.

  2. Choose videos. Tick the video posts you want to include. Only your own posts can be registered.

  3. Your consent. Choose what may be used (video, audio, transcript, face, voice), for which purposes, by whom and until when. You can state what compensation you expect; consentvid does not pay or enforce it. You confirm that you recorded the video or hold the rights, that you are an adult, and who appears in it.

  4. Review and sign. The page first downloads each video once to check it against the post and to look for location or device metadata. That metadata cannot be removed without changing the post, so such videos are refused unless you tick “include anyway”. The alternative is to delete the post and post a cleaned copy.

02

Consent page: upload from your computer

For a video file that is not posted anywhere.

  1. Sign in as in 01 and choose “Upload from my computer”.

  2. Choose or drop files of up to 300 MB each. Some servers accept less: one left at the reference default accepts 5 MiB. Give each file a title. Titles are published; file names are not.

  3. Your browser removes identifying metadata before uploading, without re-encoding. WebM, MKV, AVI and MPEG cannot be cleaned in the browser. Convert them to MP4, use the command line, or tick “include anyway” to upload them unchanged.

  4. Choose your terms and sign, as in 01.

Nothing is posted to your feed, but the file is public: anyone who has its address can download it, and datasets link to it. The video record stores it as a file reference (#pdsBlobRef) in place of a post reference.

03

Command line and Python

Scripts, many files at once, and storage beyond your own server.

  1. Clone github.com/ekazakos/consentvid, install it with the commands under Building a dataset, and log in with an app password.

  2. Each file is stripped with ffmpeg before upload unless you pass --keep-metadata. With --from-post, the post’s video is checked as in 01 instead.

  3. --storage-kind chooses where the file goes: pds (as in 02), r2, ipfs, peertube or url. Without it the configured default is used. If none is configured the file stays local, and a shared dataset cannot use a local file.

  4. contribute_many works through files one at a time, paced under your server’s write limits, and resumes when re-run.

04

Bluesky bot

Tag a video post; the bot replies with a link into 01.

  1. Post a video with #consentvid or mentioning @consentvid.org, or reply to your own video post mentioning @consentvid.org.

  2. The bot replies once, within about a minute, with a link that opens the consent page with that video already selected.

  3. Sign in as the post’s author and continue as in 01.

The bot posts from its own account and never writes to yours; the records are written when you sign in. It ignores mentions under other people’s videos, and keeps only a list of the posts it has answered and a daily reply count per author.

What becomes public

Consent and video records are public by design: anyone can read them and check the signature, and network relays copy them as they are written. They contain what you enter or choose, plus what the tools measure from the file. Anyone can build a dataset from them, and consentvid does not choose who does.

Deleting a record withdraws consent, and indexes that follow the protocol drop it. Copies made earlier can persist.

Building a dataset

No account is needed. There is no blanket licence: each clip may be used only within the terms of its own consent record, and anything not listed is not granted. See the terms of use.

export writes a Croissant 1.0 manifest with responsible-AI fields. resync fetches each record from the contributor’s own server with its signed commit and Merkle proof, and checks the signature against the account’s key, so relays, mirrors and caches do not have to be trusted. The account’s host usually holds that key (see Limitations).

Only clips reported valid are kept in the cleaned manifest. --include-labeled keeps labelled clips. --lenient keeps clips that could not be checked (unknown); use it only on manifests you built yourself, since a contributor’s server can make its clips uncheckable, and the terms of use require dropping them. To cite a dataset, use the citeAs field of its manifest.

What resync reports

Record schemas: org.consentvid.videoV2 · org.consentvid.consentV2. A sample manifest in the version 1 format; its records were withdrawn, so resync drops every clip. Version 1 schemas: org.consentvid.video, org.consentvid.consent.

Known limitations

Full trust model in the README
A signature is not proof of truth.
It shows which account made a statement, not that the person filmed the clip, is an adult, or that people in it agreed. Capture-time provenance (C2PA bound to the account) is not verified yet.
The hosting server usually holds the key.
On bsky.social and most hosts, the server operator holds the account’s signing key. Verification makes you independent of relays and mirrors, not of the host.
Withdrawal does not reach existing copies.
Datasets downloaded before a withdrawal keep their files until their users run resync. The terms of use require them to do that and to delete withdrawn clips. Under the terms, a model trained on a clip that resync reported as valid at the time does not have to be destroyed after a later withdrawal, following GDPR Art. 7(3). No new use of a withdrawn clip is allowed.
Metadata removal does not touch the picture.
Faces, number plates and burnt-in timestamps stay. In the browser only MP4 and MOV are rewritten, and data inside the video frames (H.264/HEVC user-data SEI) is not removed; the command line removes it with ffmpeg. Metadata already in a Bluesky post cannot be removed without changing the post.
Deleting your account does not delete every copy.
Your server deletes its own files with the account. Bytes on R2, IPFS or PeerTube need consentvid revoke --delete-bytes first, and IPFS copies pinned by others cannot be recalled.
Nothing enforces the terms technically.
They bind only dataset builders who choose to follow them and to run resync.
No way yet for a third party to dispute a clip.
Someone who appears in a video, or holds rights to it, cannot yet attach a signed objection. A labeler for this is planned.

Planned, not built

Next: a labeler for disputes

An ATproto labeler is a separate account that publishes signed labels about records. consentvid would run one so that a person who appears in a clip, or a rights holder, can dispute it with a label such as disputed-bystander. resync and export would then drop labelled clips, as they already drop clips that carry the contributor’s own content labels. The contributor’s consent record would not be changed.

Reviewing disputes needs moderators and a written review policy, so this depends on funding. Until then, report the account to its hosting server or, on Bluesky, with Report account. If the account is taken down, resync reports its clips as unavailable and drops them. You can also email, though an email cannot remove a record from someone else’s account.
